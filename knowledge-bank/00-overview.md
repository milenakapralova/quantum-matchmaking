# What the EQTA is

Booklet pp. 3–8, 11–15, 17–18, 29, 101–103.

Quantum technology is treated as a key technology: it is entering products and services, computers, sensors, and networks, with large effects on society, the economy, and science. Some applications already exist, some will arrive in the coming years, and some still need major technical breakthroughs. The direction of that development is partly set by the opportunities organizations and society choose to pursue. Seizing those opportunities, and putting the conditions for responsible use in place in time, takes a focused effort.

The Exploratory Quantum Technology Assessment is the practical roadmap for that effort. An organization uses it to look early at opportunities and impact, avoid negative effects, and set preconditions so that both can be managed.

The word **exploratory** is deliberate. Quantum technology is moving quickly, and so are the conditions around it: security, privacy, data and cloud law, standardization, and geopolitics (p. 3, note).

## What it helps an organization do

- See what quantum technology may bring, and whether that is desirable.
- Test ideas and decide what is needed to realize them.
- Choose which developments to follow, and whether to start or steer them.
- Decide how much hands-on experience the organization needs.
- Give management, staff, and other stakeholders a shared picture and involve them in shaping ideas.
- Identify the relevant aspects, take a decision, justify it, and set up a process that revisits the decision when new information arrives.
- After the assessment, cooperate with others, work on standards, ethical frameworks, or legislation, watch geopolitical effects, push education toward the skills that will be needed, and push suppliers toward services that fit.

The annex treats the last step, evaluation, as perhaps the most important part: set a moment to test the decision, look at the effect of the measures, and plan the next steps (pp. 101–103).

## Two kinds of organization

1. Organizations that feel the impact of quantum technology now, or will feel it as adoption grows.
2. Organizations that develop or apply quantum technology themselves, now or later.

Typical people who start the process: quantum-application frontrunners, innovation managers, chief security officers, chief information officers, lawyers, and ethicists. They cannot finish it alone. Decisions are complex, uncertainty is real, and organizational goals still have to be met. The dialogue has to include management, the people who will work with the technology, the people and organizations who feel its impact, and, where the issue requires it, service providers, technology providers, and specialists in the societal, technical, legal, or ethical side. Who sits in the room depends on the nature and impact of the application.

## Three parts

The full roadmap is a Quick Scan, an exploratory part, and a practical part.

| Part | Contents | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Quick Scan | Technology and timelines; applications; a short impact description; a short stakeholder analysis; a dialogue | Decide whether active guidance is needed |
| Exploration | Step 1 social, Step 2 technological, Step 3 legal and ethical | Build the picture from different perspectives |
| In practice | Step 4 dialogue, Step 5 trade-off, Step 6 record, Step 7 review | Turn the picture into a justified, revisitable decision |

The Quick Scan dialogue uses the same method as Step 4. If that session shows that action or active monitoring is needed, the organization continues at Step 1 and goes deeper on the societal, technical, legal, and ethical areas that matter. If active guidance is not needed, that decision is also written down and justified.

## How to walk the steps

The steps look at the same issues from different interests, so overlap is expected. Avoid repeating work, and still do justice to each perspective. Some questions cannot be answered yet because the application idea is still broad. The response is to install a process that picks up relevant developments in time, not to force an answer.

## Design principles

The working group that wrote the EQTA joined three worlds: people who create the technology, innovation managers and quantum pioneers in organizations that will use it or be affected by it, and lawyers and ethicists. The integration principles were constructive, participatory, bottom-up, and practical. The text is meant to stay in accessible language and to stay tied to real applications.

The preface’s reason for doing this early comes from lessons of AI: at the stage when pioneers are still exploring an application area, widening the view to the societal, ethical, legal, and technical context is what makes it possible to seize opportunities and avoid harm. Stakeholder engagement is the cited lesson (*Lessons from AI: stakeholder engagement*, QDNL, 2022).

## What the EQTA is not

- It is not a substitute for a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) or a Human Rights and Algorithms Impact Assessment (IAMA). Those may be done as well. If quantum technology is used to build better machine-learning models, the IAMA is the additional tool aimed at negative effects of algorithms.
- It is not a checklist of right and wrong, or of allowed and forbidden. Judging a technology as such invites total embrace or total rejection. The EQTA starts from the entanglement of technology, people, organization, and society, and asks how a specific application in a specific context can be developed and used responsibly.
- It does not tell the user what is responsible. It gives a way to reach that judgment.
- It is limited in scope. Organizations will have questions it does not answer. The Centre for Quantum and Society is the first place to take those questions (p. 30).
- It is not, in this 2023 edition, a binding legal instrument.

Its added value, as the text states it, is an early and structured way to consider the impact of quantum technologies (p. 30).
