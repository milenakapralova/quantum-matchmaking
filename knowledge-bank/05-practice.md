# Steps 4–7 — In practice

Booklet pp. 15, 68–71, and the Quick Scan dialogue on pp. 6–8 and 15.

The EQTA refuses a checklist of right and wrong. Treating a technology as simply good or bad leads to embracing or condemning it wholesale, and suggests that technology and society are opponents. The document’s starting point is that they are densely connected. Technology can improve health care and at the same time create privacy harms. The useful question is not whether the technology in general is responsible. It is how this application, in this context, can be developed and used responsibly.

## Step 4 — Stakeholder dialogue

The dialogue is the last step of the Quick Scan and the fourth step of the full roadmap. The method is the same. The ambition differs.

- In the Quick Scan, the dialogue forms a quick view of which aspects are relevant and whether a more precise analysis is needed. The reader then either stops or returns to Step 1.
- After Steps 1 to 3, the reader has concerns and requirements from the social, technical, organizational, legal, and ethical perspectives. Step 4 brings them together.

People in the room:

- experts who can create an understanding of the opportunities and who know the technical, social, ethical, and legal frameworks;
- administrators who steer the organization;
- stakeholders who experience the impact, and the people in the organization who will work with the technology.

The purpose is a tangible approach for the organization, including advantages and disadvantages, built from those perspectives. The creative conversation is how concerns from domains that usually stay separate become concrete approaches.

### Guidance ethics

The dialogue uses ECP’s Approach to Guidance Ethics (figure 05). Three stages:

| Stage | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1. Case | The technology in its context |
| 2. Dialogue | Actors, values, the environment, effects, and the user |
| 3. Options for action | Concrete measures |

Describe the application in context as concretely as possible. Step 2 of the roadmap has already started that description. The more concrete the case, the more accurately impact on people and society can be mapped.

Then identify everyone involved, the positive and negative effects on them, and the values at stake.

Then name concrete approaches: measures that mitigate negative effects or strengthen positive ones. Measures may change the technology, the context, or the way the technology is used. All stakeholders should be in that discussion.

The shift the method is meant to produce: from “is this technology responsible?” to “how do we develop and use it responsibly?”

## Step 5 — Considerations and assessment

Trade-offs use four mechanisms from the legal toolbox. They are also used for the combined legal, social, and ethical impact.

**Effectiveness.** Will the proposed action sufficiently achieve the goal? That requires foresight: how likely is it that deploying the quantum application will actually achieve the stated goal or goals?

**Necessity and subsidiarity.** Look at every tool available to achieve the goal. Then ask whether the intended application is the least intrusive means of achieving it.

**Proportionality.** Weigh the impact of the application on the individual against the importance of the societal goals. A more important goal makes more permissible.

The short roadmap on p. 15 lists subsidiarity, proportionality, and necessity, in that order, and does not name effectiveness. The full chapter on p. 70 leads with effectiveness and pairs necessity with subsidiarity. For a real assessment, use the four tests in the full chapter.

## Step 6 — Commitment and accountability

Record the outcomes. The record does two jobs: it is the reference and the assurance for design and further development, and it helps substantiate the decisions and considerations afterwards. Annex 2 is the documentation overview. The fill-in sheet is the downloadable roadmap on quantumdelta.nl, not a form printed in the PDF.

The short name on p. 15 is “decision making, recording, and justification.” The chapter heading is “commitment and accountability.” They are the same step.

## Step 7 — Feedback and periodic evaluation

The application will often still be under development. Periodically reopen the ethical and social justification. Regular evaluation is a feedback loop that improves the application and makes it more effective. The step should state how that loop will be achieved.

The annex (pp. 101–103) goes further on what the loop is for. After the EQTA, organizations start cooperating, work on standards, ethical frameworks, and legislation, watch geopolitical effects on their ideas, press education to supply skills in time, and press service providers to build what they actually need. The most important part of going through the EQTA may be this last step: plan a moment to test the decision, look at the effect of the measures, and plan further steps.

## What “done” looks like

A finished pass produces all of the following:

- a concrete description of the application and its context;
- the social, technical, legal, and ethical conditions and gaps;
- one or more approaches, with advantages and disadvantages, tested for effectiveness, necessity, subsidiarity, and proportionality;
- a written decision and the reasons for it, including the decision to stop after the Quick Scan;
- a stated process for review, with a moment already planned.
