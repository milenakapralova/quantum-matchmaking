# Step 3 — Legal and ethical framework

Booklet pp. 14, 53–67.

Legal and ethical frameworks belong together. Legislation is sometimes “solidified ethics”: norms, values, and principles turned into laws and rules of conduct. The legal framework then supplies the context for ethical judgment. Ethical commitments can also stand on their own, with no prospect of becoming law. Sustainability choices and employee benefits are the examples. An organization can bind itself to ethical goals and be held to account for them. Many organizations use the Sustainable Development Goals as that guide and report plans and progress in the annual report.

Ethical trade-offs are also required when legislation is inadequate. With new technology, law either lags or has to be read again. Kop, *Establishing a Legal-Ethical Framework for Quantum Technology* (Yale Journal of Law & Technology, The Record, 2021), is the cited argument.

Figure 02, taken from IEEE’s *Ethically Aligned Design* (2017), draws the relationship as values and norms and ethical principles leading toward laws and regulations, with both feeding wellbeing and human rights.

Legal frameworks set constraints and limit room for maneuver, so they are examined on their own as well as beside ethics. The overview in the document is a starting direction, not a complete list. It separates:

- general law, applying across sectors;
- technology-specific law;
- sector-specific law.

## General law

General frameworks apply regardless of application or sector. The families named are human rights, fundamental freedoms, economic and social rights, civil law, criminal law, administrative law, and tax law. The text then highlights three.

**Human rights.** Basic rights, fundamental rights, and human rights are treated as interchangeable terms: legal norms that assure personal freedoms and a dignified existence, and that apply to all people regardless of nationality. Sources listed: the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, the European Convention on Human Rights, the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU, the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (the document abbreviates it IVESC), and the constitution.

**Data protection.** The AVG is described as the Dutch implementation of the GDPR. It sets rules for processing personal data by companies and government, strengthens privacy rights, and gives organizations more responsibility. Separate rules cover police and justice: the Police Data Act (Wpg) and the Judicial and Criminal Records Act (Wjsg).

**Public administration.** Public bodies owe duties of conduct toward citizens and businesses. Some sit in the General Administrative Law Act (Awb). Others are unwritten and are called the general principles of proper administration (the document abbreviates them Abbb).

## Intellectual property

Geopolitical tension and recent technology are said to put patents and other intellectual property under close scrutiny. The analysis cited for quantum technology is Aboy, Minssen, and Kop, “Mapping the Patent Landscape of Quantum Technologies,” *IIC* 53 (2022).

Later, in the medical-device illustration, the EQTA is also a way for a multidisciplinary team to see which rights arise and must be registered: patents, copyrights, and trade secrets. A technology-transfer agreement with a university may need review. Optimizing the patent portfolio is said to raise the value of a medical device, and enforcement can reduce counterfeiting. If third-party intellectual property is used, the EQTA report should record that it must be licensed.

## No quantum-specific statute yet

As of this document, there is no legislation specifically regulating the social opportunities of quantum technology or the prevention of its negative impacts. The first question is whether the quantum application stands alone or is combined with, or used for, another technology such as AI or biotechnology.

Specific rules for AI and data already exist, including the AVG. The European AI Act is described as work underway in Brussels. The proposal divides AI systems, products, and services into four risk categories:

1. Prohibited applications, such as government social scoring.
2. High-risk applications, such as HR systems, which face specific legal requirements. CE marking, required before market introduction, is named as an important compliance tool for high-risk systems.
3. Limited risk.
4. Minimal risk.

As risk rises, requirements become stricter, up to a prohibition. The document’s further reading is Kop, “EU Artificial Intelligence Act: The European Approach to AI” (2021).

## United States, and what the EU might do next

The text contrasts Brussels leading on AI regulation with Washington adopting quantum-specific regulation. It dates two presidential directives to May 2021, and a national security memorandum to May of the surrounding period. The footnote URL is the White House fact sheet of 4 May 2022. Read the dates as the document’s, and check the primary source before relying on them.

As the document describes US policy: one aim is to support domestic market leadership by encouraging innovation; the other is to stop the technology reaching the wrong hands and threatening economic stability and national security. The memorandum is said to bring export controls on certain quantum technologies, action against intellectual-property theft, and work to reduce cybercrime risks.

The AI Act’s risk-based approach is described as a good fit for quantum technology because most quantum technologies are **dual-use**: usable for civilian and military purposes. The document’s illustration is nuclear technology, medical isotopes on one side and the atomic bomb on the other. Quantum’s distinctive properties are also said to need a tailor-made approach.

From that, the document expects, within a few years of publication:

- EU legislation specific to quantum technology, in the form of a Quantum Governance Act;
- possibly an international Quantum Treaty;
- an important role for standardization, certification, and lifecycle auditing by means of binding Exploratory Quantum Technology Assessments;
- possible EU export controls, affecting research, trade in quantum technology and materials, and the fragile software and hardware supply chain;
- demanding requirements on funding for quantum research and development.

These are expectations in a 2023 text. They are not a description of law in force at the time of writing.

## Quantum-ELSPI

The practical consequence for EQTA users is to stay aware of the ethical, legal, social, and policy implications of quantum technology. The document calls this **Quantum-ELSPI**. The tool can be used to identify current and future legal requirements and to anticipate them, covering both compliance and intellectual-property opportunities and duties. Further ELSPI questions are referred to the *Digital Society* collection “Quantum ELSPI” (Springer Nature, 2021). A transatlantic view is referred to Stanford Law School’s “Regulating Quantum Technology” project.

## Sector and product law

Examples, not a closed list:

- Financial sector: the Financial Supervision Act (Wft) and the Anti-Money Laundering and Anti-Terrorist Financing Act (Wwft).
- Telecommunications Act.
- Patient rights: the Medical Treatment Agreement Act (WGBO).
- Medical devices: the Medical Device Regulation.
- Product safety, including the Product Safety Directive and the Machinery Regulation.

Doing business in the United States or Asia brings the local law of that place on top.

### Illustration: a quantum-infused medical device

A device marketed in Europe that also uses AI or machine learning and classical data is, legally, a quantum/AI hybrid. General law, technology-specific frameworks for AI, data, and quantum, and the healthcare frameworks all apply. Once the relevant European laws are passed, the document says at least the following would bear on the device:

- the EU AI Act;
- the Quantum Governance Act;
- the laws of the European strategy for data (GDPR, free flow of data, Data Act, Data Governance Act, and others);
- the Medical Device Regulation;
- the Database Directive;
- relevant intellectual-property directives;
- possibly export controls under the Wassenaar Arrangement;
- Dutch law.

The Quantum Governance Act appears here as forthcoming law, consistent with the forecast above.

## Security and privacy

Quantum communications and a future quantum internet sharpen an existing conflict between security and privacy.

On one side, sensitive information of governments and citizens should stay secure and confidential. On the other, national security, and sometimes investigation and prosecution, make it desirable or necessary to intercept some communications. The document locates that tension in the Intelligence and Security Services Act (Wiv) and the debate and referendum around it, the Network and Information Systems Security Act (also called the Cybersecurity Act in this passage), Computer Crime III, and the Article 29 Working Party’s position on encryption.

Public concern is evidenced, in the text, by the Wiv debate, the referendum, and growing use of encrypted VPNs. The Network and Information Systems Security Act and the GDPR require appropriate security measures, of which encryption is an example. At the same time, providers of public telecommunications networks and services may only make them available to users if they are interceptable under Chapter 13 of the Telecommunications Act. A form of the internet that cannot be broken into looks ideal under one statute and undesirable under another. The document says potential quantum networks are no different.

## Ethical framework

Ethics, as used here, is the philosophical study of doing the right thing. It does not hand over a list of right and wrong. It offers methods for reaching a judgment. The goal of the consideration is that the finished quantum application lines up with prevailing societal views of what is ethical. The EQTA leaves the judgment to the user and supplies guidance for getting there.

Digitization, quantum technology, AI, and cloud computing raise ethical questions, often in the form “is it acceptable to deploy this?” Law and ethics are related: social values are translated into norms, laws, and rules, and law sets the leeway for deployment. They are treated separately because the user’s starting point differs. In the legal frame, the statute is the test. In the ethical frame, societal values are the starting point. Law takes time and lags technical development, so the legal frame often does not yet give enough guidance and an ethical consideration is useful.

The cited method guide is ECP’s Approach to Guidance Ethics, used in Step 4.

### World Economic Forum principles

*Quantum Computing Governance Principles* (World Economic Forum, 2022) is the guide the document uses for turning ethical concerns about quantum computing into governance. The scope warning is explicit: the principles apply to quantum computing, not to the whole family of quantum technology.

The WEF path, as summarized: identify stakeholders by their interests, then core values and themes; within each theme identify objectives, opportunities, and negative impacts; those become the basis for principles and actions.

Stakeholder groups in the WEF figure: governments and universities; corporations and private entities that use the technology; corporations and private entities that develop it; developers; consumers; academics and international organizations.

Core values the EQTA draws out:

| Value | What the document asks |
|---|---|
| Common good | Analyze how the application can contribute to the wellbeing of society. Harness it to benefit humanity. |
| Accountability | Design and implement so the organization can be held to account for impacts and can manage them: mitigate harm, strengthen opportunities. Cover design, use, and results. Society also needs institutions that sanction organizations that evade accountability or fail to act when consequences are negative. |
| Inclusivity | Include a diverse range of stakeholder views. Aim at meaningful dialogue and avoid narrow definitions of harm and benefit. |
| Equitability | Distribute benefits and impacts fairly across groups. Pay particular attention to the needs of vulnerable populations. |
| Non-maleficence | Use the technology so that it does not harm people, intentionally or not. Collect impact data systematically. Complying with a legal duty is not enough: organizations that do exactly what the law requires can still cause unintended harm. The National Ombudsman and the Kafka Brigade are the examples. Gather information on effects and intervene when the impact was not intended. |
| Accessibility | Knowledge of quantum technology, and access to facilities such as quantum computing as a service, should be widely available across development, application, and use. That implies standardization and the avoidance of monopoly or dependence on one party or country. The market should allow new entrants. The technology should eventually be affordable and usable by everyone with an interest. This can conflict with governments’ military-strategic interest and with large parties that can buy and control the market. It also implies regulation, agreements on sharing and reusing data, and effective control of data by stakeholders. Purchasers should not assume that the large, cheap vendor is in the long-term interest of the organization, the sector, or society. |
| Transparency | Users, developers, and regulators are transparent about purpose and intention. |

The principles figure also names themes that cut across those values: transformative capabilities, sustainability, access to hardware, standardization, infrastructure, privacy, open innovation, cybersecurity, awareness, and workforce development and capability-building. The figure is a graphic; this list is the labels that can be read from it, not a ranking.

### Choosing another framework

The WEF principles are not the only ethical framework. Many quantum-ethics initiatives are under development. Ethics is dynamic, culturally sensitive, and specific to context. An organization may choose another framework if a clear scientific method underlies it and it comes from a reliable source. The sources named are the UN, the OECD, Asilomar, and the IEEE. A technology ethicist can help. The framework should be as specific to quantum technology as possible, and then supplemented with principles, values, goals, and themes relevant to the particular system. Kop’s *Physics World* essay of 17 December 2021 is the citation. The document again stresses doing this in a multidisciplinary team.
