# Step 1 — Social framework

Booklet pp. 13, 36–47.

A quantum application has to work in society, not only in the lab. Legislation, infrastructure, and public opinion sit largely outside any one organization. Step 1 makes that wider setting visible. Organizations can shape some of those processes, and they also depend on other actors for how quantum technology reaches practice.

## System technology, with a warning

The step borrows from the Scientific Council for Government Policy (WRR) report on AI, *Opgave AI* / *Mission AI* (2021). The WRR calls AI a **system technology**: a technology that enables innovation across many fields and therefore has a systemic impact. Electricity, the internal combustion engine, and the internet are the examples. Quantum technology is placed in that family, and the WRR’s lessons from earlier system technologies are reused.

Some members of the EQTA working group disagree with the label for both AI and quantum technology. The risk they name: “system technology” suggests a break with the past, as if impact analysis were needed only now. The same impacts often already arise from earlier technology. Stressing the break can produce rules that catch a problem in the new technology and miss the same problem in technology already in use. The social and ethical attention the EQTA asks for is therefore also a plea to look critically at existing information technology.

## Five tasks

If a new system technology is to be embedded responsibly and successfully, the WRR says five things matter. The EQTA turns them into five aspects of the social context of a quantum application (its second “Figure 01”):

| Task | EQTA name | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Demystification | Perception | What are the perceptions surrounding quantum technology? |
| Contextualization | Socio-technical ecosystem | What are the social and technical conditions for success? |
| Engagement | Stakeholders | Who is impacted? |
| Regulation | Frameworks | What frameworks apply? |
| Positioning | International context | What is the international context? |

Perception, the ecosystem, stakeholders, and regulation each have an international dimension. The international context is still treated as its own aspect.

The success of an application is partly decided by this context. Organizations can work on each task themselves.

### Perception

Ideas about how a new system technology works, and what it can do, often do not match reality. Excessive expectations produce disillusion and poorly considered applications. Exaggerated fears produce aversion and missed opportunities. Demystification means replacing myths with a realistic view of what the technology can and cannot do, so people can think about opportunities and negative effects.

Quantum technology is exposed to myths because it is both complex and full of promise. The quantum computer is the example: it is sometimes imagined as better than a classical computer in every respect. The document calls that unfair. The promise is an advantage for certain kinds of problems. The technology is also still unknown, and will stay partly incomprehensible for a long time, including for experts. That can become lack of interest, and then society does not debate the opportunities or the harms. Not everyone needs to know how it works. What matters is that unrealistic perceptions do not block application, and that people can take part in thinking about applications.

For an organization about to apply it: map existing perceptions and how they may affect the intended use; watch the organization’s own wording; decide who needs to know what; then build that knowledge inside the organization or, with others, in society. The primer’s stance on language is in [07-quantum-primer.md](07-quantum-primer.md).

### Socio-technical ecosystem

A favorable ecosystem is one where both social and technical surroundings help the technology function. People who will use it need the skills, and organizational processes have to fit. Supporting technology and infrastructure have to be there. The WRR’s illustration is the self-driving car: it works in practice only if occupants and other drivers can use it, and if the road becomes more readable through smart signs. Performance of the device alone is not enough.

For quantum technology the document stresses supporting hardware and software. Quantum computers need quantum chips, suitable programming languages, and algorithms for particular problems. Experts expect quantum computing power, and simulators, to be available mainly as a cloud service. Quantum Inspire from QuTech Delft is the example (quantum-inspire.com). Success then depends partly on access to those services. A single organization often cannot create these conditions. It can call on others or offer support.

Ask what the application requires of people and of the way of working, and what technical ecosystem it assumes. Ask how a change in that environment would make the application work better. The cited background is Hoofnagle and Garfinkel, *Law and Policy for the Quantum Age* (Cambridge University Press, 2022).

### Stakeholders

Engagement means involving stakeholders during development, implementation, and feedback. The WRR’s phrase is to “democratize” the technology: give different groups a voice in design and evaluation. Development is often dominated by technical considerations and by the interests of developers. Involvement improves anticipation of undesirable effects and increases support.

Quantum technology’s specialist character and high cost create a risk that development stays with a small group of specialists. Using the potential, and limiting undesirable effects, requires other groups to contribute ideas during development, implementation, and evaluation. Making the technology and its forms accessible is described as part of QDNL’s mission.

Ask who is impacted and how, and how those groups can be involved beforehand and structurally, so the impacts stay visible. De Jong, *Own the Unknown* (Digital Society, 2022), is the cited source on anticipating the social side of quantum technology.

### Regulatory frameworks

Regulation is every rule and guiding framework that defines the room in which a technology may be developed and applied. Frameworks often already exist and can be applied, sometimes after modification. Sometimes the technology creates situations that have not been seen before, and new rules are needed before anyone has much experience. The WRR then points to flexible instruments such as standards and norms. Regulation is a balance between leaving room and setting rules that steer development and use.

How quantum technology meets existing law, including intellectual property, is Step 3. Standards are already being developed, including for post-quantum cryptography and cybersecurity. Present and emerging frameworks are part of the social context because they partly decide how the technology can be used.

An organization should ask which regulations apply. The answer may be that adequate rules are missing, which can undermine legal certainty and allow abuse. Some frameworks are professional. Lawyers, accountants, doctors, civil servants, and notaries carry duties that can conflict with a technology, or that the technology could perform better. The document’s examples of outdated requirements are a “wet” signature and physical presence to prove identity when a reliable digital proof already exists.

Some applications need new agreements and sometimes new institutions. The example is a system authority that lets organizations exchange sensitive data easily and reliably inside the applicable rules. That requires cooperation among organizations, regulators, and professional bodies such as auditors. Depending on the framework, an organization develops it itself or puts it on another profession’s agenda.

Standardization returns in Step 2. To switch vendor or service provider later without heavy reinvestment, users and providers need high-quality standards early, able to anticipate later developments and carrying broad support.

### International context

Development of a new system technology is often framed as an international race with one winner. The WRR calls that frame simplistic and misleading: one party’s gain is not necessarily another’s loss. Strategic thought about one’s own position still matters, especially for competitiveness and security.

Quantum research is highly international because it is specialized: experts from many countries work together on breakthroughs. It is also very expensive, so a small number of players dominate worldwide. Application therefore almost always has an international component.

That field is increasingly shaped by **sovereignty**: geopolitical and technological self-determination of countries and groups of countries, including the Netherlands and the EU. From that desire can follow export and import restrictions, limits on working with people and organizations from some countries outside Europe, and limits on foreign ownership. The wish for sovereignty sometimes conflicts with the international research community.

Ask which players are involved, which dependencies may arise, whether foreign actors have access to the application, and what the implications are.
