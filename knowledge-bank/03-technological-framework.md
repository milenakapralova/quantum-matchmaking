# Step 2 — Technological framework

Booklet pp. 13, 48–53.

Impact on people and society cannot be judged from the application idea alone. Quantum technology is becoming part of applications in which data are central, so the technology has generic requirements. The document groups them as functionality, reliability, and quality; interoperability; how the technology handles data (identity, authentication, authorization, and control); and how values around that handling are read, including the prevention of monopoly control over data.

European legislation expected in the years after the report will regulate important parts of these requirements. Step 2 therefore sits close to Step 3. The laws named are the Cybersecurity Act, the Data Governance Act, the AI Act, and the General Data Protection Regulation. Putting them into practice often requires collaboration and fundamental technical choices.

## Quality

Question 6 asks which quality requirements the technology must meet to achieve the stated goals and benefits.

Functionality is the start: what the technology can do and which functions it offers. Around that sit robustness against errors, the ability to analyze and repair errors, securing systems, flexibility, manageability, and the possibility of integration with other technology. Sustainability counts as well: origin and reuse of raw materials, and energy consumption.

ISO/IEC 25010 is the suggested source for a complete list of software and system quality requirements. The document notes that these quality issues are usually handled by requiring certifications from the vendor.

## Privacy, security, data, and identity

Question 7 asks which technical requirements should be imposed for privacy, security, data, identity, authentication, and authorization.

The working questions:

- Which technical principles protect privacy and give data subjects appropriate opportunities for consent and control?
- Which authentication and authorization mechanisms let the many data subjects control collection and use?
- How will reliability and security of data be ensured?
- Powerful quantum computing will require a lot of data, and parties will have to make data available to each other. Choices are required about which data is cooperative, which is competitive, which is confidential, and which is open. Joining or creating a management organization may be necessary to safeguard security, privacy, and quality.
- How can the risks of growing interconnectedness, and of dependence on third parties, be managed?

### Identity

Identity solutions for responsible handling of personal data are described as still in the pipeline. The examples are the forthcoming European e-wallet and the principles of self-sovereign identity.

### Information security and continuity

Quantum technology is often bought as a service. The software keeps changing, many parties develop it, and many parties collect and share data. Technical interconnectedness and mutual dependence increase. That calls for targeted management of information-security and continuity risks.

The financial sector is described as leading, with parties exchanging information on risks, measures, and effectiveness under the International Standards for Assurance Engagements (ISAE). In the Netherlands, the Online Trust Coalition works to apply those principles to security and continuity in complex networks.

### Interoperability

On data interoperability, the document points to the FAIR principles: findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable. Automatically understanding and combining data from many sources, and keeping effective control, requires standardization of interoperability.

## Future-proof investment and freedom of choice

Question 8 asks which preconditions keep investments future-proof and preserve the organization’s freedom of choice.

New technology has a phase in which a few parties dominate because they are first to market. The organizational question is when to jump in. The considerations named are whether the investment will still hold, and whether proprietary standards will create vendor lock-in and remove the freedom to make later technical choices. Many new technologies use standards owned by one developer, which makes it hard to move to or integrate another vendor’s technology.

Users should therefore seek cooperation with other users and with vendors early, to reach open standards for quantum applications. NEN, the Royal Netherlands Standardization Institute, is involved in many of these international processes. Under certain conditions NEN can start a new process, and it can connect organizations to standardization committees.

Suppliers often join standardization so the standard leaves room for later developments and fits their strategy. The existence of a standard also signals maturity to customers. That confidence creates demand, which helps technology development, and it lets later innovators enter an existing market more quickly.
